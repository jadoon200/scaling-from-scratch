Scaling from Scratch

Three ML-systems projects built from first principles in Apple MLX — not to make a model that works, but to understand where the hardware time actually goes: FLOPs, memory bandwidth, scaling laws, and the GPU kernels themselves.

Project 01 — mlx-tiny-transformer

A transformer, and its scaling law

A LLaMA-style decoder (RoPE · RMSNorm · SwiGLU · GQA · KV-cache), trained on FineWeb-Edu, then swept to fit the Chinchilla law empirically.

Loss falls cleanly with compute, and each model size traces its own curve. Note the crossover: at low compute the smaller model wins — a bigger model only pays off once it has enough data.

At small scale the token-embedding table dominates the parameter budget (85% at 1M → 25% at 100M), which is exactly why these models are memory-bound. Scaling laws therefore track non-embedding params.

The converged 10M model produces real, grammatical English (top-p decode through the KV cache):

“Scientists have discovered that the majority of the global climate, though many of them have been developed in recent years…”

“The history of the three noble is a poor and good had a most attractive and vast life-based property…”

Locally coherent rather than globally — expected for a 10M model on a 45M-token slice — but real text, not noise.

Project 02 — mlx-quantization

Quantization: where the speed actually comes from

From-scratch group-wise INT8/INT4 weight quantization, benchmarked on the quality ↔ speed ↔ memory tradeoff.

The lesson in one chart. At batch=1 decode the bottleneck is reading weights from memory. But a naive dequantize-then-matmul is ~9× slower than fp16 — it expands the weight back to float first. Only a fused kernel that reads packed weights directly wins (1.9× / 2.9×). Speed comes from the kernel, not the data format.

And the quality cost is almost nothing — perplexity is flat across precisions thanks to group-wise scales, so the Pareto-optimal pick is INT4 with a large group: smallest, no measurable loss.

From-scratch QuantizedLinear matches MLX's native fused kernel to 4 decimals. Memory savings are modest because the fp16 embedding is ~half the model — the same dominance seen in Project 01.

Project 03 — mlx-metal-kernels

Hand-written Metal kernels at the bandwidth roof

Six custom GPU kernels via mx.fast.metal_kernel, benchmarked against MLX's naive paths and its hand-optimized builtins.

These ops are memory-bound, so the figure of merit is achieved bandwidth vs the M3 Pro's ~150 GB/s roof. The custom kernels reach 77–85% of peak — and two of them beat MLX's own builtins: the GEMV beats mx.matmul, and the flash-style attention beats scaled_dot_product_attention by keeping the score matrix on-chip.

The capstone — fusing Projects 02 + 03. A quantized GEMV that reads packed INT4/INT8 weights directly in the kernel, dequantizing inline. This is the kernel Project 02 proved you need: 2.6× (INT8) / 2.4× (INT4) faster decode than fp32.
